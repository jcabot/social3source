\begin{abstract}
Software can be created and deployed faster than ever, but what is the point if it does not contribute to society?
The goal of building software should be to improve human lives.
This requires more than publishing it as open source: it requires considering how it is developed, the qualities it embodies, and the purposes it serves.
We introduce \socialsource{}, which connects social provenance, societal qualities, and social purpose on an open-source foundation.
This position paper defines the concept, illustrates it through a hypothetical application for migrant services, and proposes a model-based development framework and research roadmap.
As our capacity to generate software grows, we should ensure that the result is worth creating.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Creating software is easier than ever thanks to technologies such as low-code development~\cite{Cabot2024LowCode} and, especially, recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI).
Visual abstractions, reusable components, and natural-language interaction help developers translate ideas into running applications with less effort.\footnote{Whether all this software meets appropriate standards of quality is a separate discussion.}
They also make development more accessible beyond the profession, supporting \emph{citizen development}, in which non-IT professionals create software~\cite{Binzer2022Citizen}, and \emph{end-user development}, in which users adapt and build software for their own needs~\cite{Fischer2004MetaDesign}.

This \emph{development fever}, however, comes at a sustainability cost.
As emphasized by the Karlskrona Manifesto, software sustainability involves interconnected environmental, economic, and social concerns, alongside individual and technical ones~\cite{Becker2015Sustainability}.
Creating, deploying, and operating more applications consumes resources, introduces maintenance and financial commitments, and can reproduce biases or reinforce exclusion.
Lowering the cost of creating an application does not eliminate the costs of sustaining it or the consequences of its use.
We should therefore assess the software's benefits to society alongside its costs and potential harms.
Paraphrasing Fei-Fei Li\footnote{\url{https://x.com/drfeifei/status/2102508817653379411}}:
\begin{quote}
The goal of building any software, AI included, should be bettering human lives and society.
\end{quote}

Open-source software grants essential freedoms to inspect, use, modify, and redistribute software~\cite{R3}.
We take these freedoms as a necessary foundation for our proposal, but they do not guarantee societal benefit.
An open license alone does not establish that development was inclusive, that an application embodies relevant societal qualities, or that its use benefits the people it aims to serve.

To connect these concerns, we introduce \socialsource{} and its three dimensions: \emph{social provenance}, \emph{societal qualities}, and \emph{social purpose}.
Together, they address how and by whom software is built, what properties it embodies, and why it exists and whom it serves.
We present the concept as a position for discussion, illustrated below, and propose a framework for integrating it into development and assessment.

The application would offer appropriate languages, accessible interaction, minimal collection of sensitive information, and support for modest devices and limited connectivity.
Accessibility evaluations, privacy checks, and testing with intended users would help establish whether these qualities are achieved in practice.

\section{The \socialsource{} concept}
\label{sec:concept}

We define \socialsource{} as \emph{open-source software that is social in how it is built, in the qualities it embodies, and in the purposes it serves}.
The name is pronounced ``Social Cubed Source''; the superscript three denotes social provenance, societal qualities, and social purpose.
Figure~\ref{fig:socialsource-foundation} shows their shared open-source foundation and interdependence.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/social3-source-foundation.png}
\caption{The three social dimensions of \socialsource{} share an open-source foundation.
The surrounding arrows emphasize their interdependence across software development, deployment, and evolution.}
\label{fig:socialsource-foundation}
\end{figure}

We propose a license approved by the Open Source Initiative (OSI) as the operational reference for the open-source foundation~\cite{R3a}.
Its freedoms allow communities to adapt an application, share improvements, and continue development beyond the involvement of its original creators.
Open governance, accessible documentation, and open standards can help communities exercise these freedoms, but require commitments beyond licensing alone.

\textbf{Social provenance: how and by whom the software is built.}
This dimension concerns the people, contributions, and decisions that shape an application throughout its development and maintenance.
It encompasses developers as well as domain experts, designers, translators, testers, users, funders, and governance participants.
The aim is to enable people with different backgrounds and relevant experience, particularly affected communities, to influence requirements and decisions through inclusive contribution routes, transparent governance, and fair recognition.

\textbf{Societal qualities: what properties the software embodies.}
This dimension concerns how an application's design and operation reflect the needs, resources, and circumstances of the people who use it.
Relevant qualities include accessibility, multilingualism, affordability, privacy, fairness, security, and environmental sustainability.
Priorities and trade-offs depend on context and should be expressed as requirements that guide implementation and evaluation.
Assessing these qualities requires examining both the implemented software and users' experience of it.

\textbf{Social purpose: why the software exists and whom it serves.}
This dimension concerns the social needs an application addresses and the consequences of its use for its intended communities.
Software may acquire such a purpose through its original design or through later adaptation and adoption by a community.
A credible account of this purpose identifies the beneficiaries and their needs, then progressively gathers evidence about adoption, benefits, and unintended harms.
Intended benefit and demonstrated impact remain distinct.

Progress in one or two dimensions is valuable; \socialsource{} encourages projects to build on it and address all three throughout the software lifecycle.
Participation can reveal overlooked accessibility needs, design choices can enable or prevent access to services, and feedback from actual use can reshape both requirements and governance.
The concept applies regardless of development technology or organizational setting, including commercial projects and non-governmental organizations (NGOs).

These commitments can require additional time and resources.
Inclusive participation needs coordination and collective decision-making; multilingual support and accessibility require design work and validation with users.
Reducing bias in AI-enabled functionality may require model selection, adaptation, or training, supported by appropriate data and evaluation.
Projects should anticipate these costs when setting their scope, resources, priorities, and minimum requirements.

\socialsource{} calls for explicit commitments and supporting evidence across all three dimensions.
Qualification thresholds, assessment methods, and the treatment of contextual trade-offs remain part of the research agenda discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Social provenance}
\label{sec:provenance}

Recognizing non-coding roles is a first step toward diversity in software development.
C\'anovas Izquierdo and Cabot~\cite{Canovas2022NonCoding} highlight their significant contribution to open-source projects.
People in both coding and non-coding roles should also have a say in decisions that shape the software.
Social provenance concerns both the range of contributions a project welcomes and the backgrounds, experiences, and perspectives represented, with relevant forms of diversity informed by the intended communities and uses.

Following the Software Diversity Card~\cite{R2}, we organize social provenance into three subdimensions: participants, usage context, and governance (Figure~\ref{fig:diversity-card}).
These capture who contributes, the communities and circumstances the software serves, and the rules and bodies that direct its development.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.68\linewidth,height=0.32\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/software-diversity-card-overview.pdf}
\caption{Overview of the Software Diversity Card, reproduced from Figure~1 of Giner-Miguelez et al.~\cite{R2}.}
\label{fig:diversity-card}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Participants.}
Participants include development teams, translators, documenters, issue reporters, and users involved in testing or feedback.
Relevant attributes include their roles, languages, experience, education, location, and demographic characteristics, where appropriate to collect and disclose.
This broader view matters because repository commits capture only part of the work, and people who report issues or join tests may not represent the intended users.
Testing conditions, including tasks, time, and working arrangements, also affect whose experience informs the result.

\textbf{Usage context.}
Usage context encompasses intended use cases, target communities, social and cultural circumstances, and adaptations for particular groups.
Relevant characteristics include language, digital literacy, accessibility needs, and the resources available to users.
These characteristics help assess whether participants bring experience relevant to the intended users and connect provenance to societal qualities and social purpose.
For the running example, language and digital-literacy differences would inform whom to involve and which adaptations to evaluate.

\textbf{Governance.}
Governance encompasses the bodies, decision-makers, and rules that direct a project, together with its organizational setting, funders, and shareholders.
For \socialsource{}, this information should help explain who prioritizes requirements, accepts contributions, and resolves disagreements.
A diverse contributor population has limited influence if it cannot shape decisions; governance records should therefore show how participation affects the application's direction.

\section{Societal qualities}
\label{sec:qualities}

Societal qualities are software properties that help realize human and social values.
Frameworks from the social sciences can help identify these properties and relate them to people's needs and circumstances.
We draw on two complementary perspectives: Schwartz's Theory of Basic Human Values and the Capability Approach.

Schwartz's Theory of Basic Human Values describes values as motivational goals and explains their compatibility and conflicts.
Its refined formulation distinguishes 19 values, covering concerns such as equality and justice, acceptance of difference, protection of nature, and autonomy of thought and action~\cite{Schwartz2012Refining}.
Table~\ref{tab:values-qualities} illustrates how selected values could inform the societal qualities and requirements of \socialsource{} software.
These mappings are our proposed interpretations.

These relationships are neither exhaustive nor one-to-one.
Privacy can support both security and autonomy, while equitable access may require accessibility, affordability, and support for limited connectivity.
Projects therefore need to justify their priorities with stakeholders and translate them into requirements suited to the intended context.
Schwartz's theory helps articulate these choices, but does not determine which values software should promote or which technical trade-offs are unavoidable.

To understand what these choices mean for users, the Capability Approach of Sen and Nussbaum directs attention to people's actual opportunities to pursue valued activities~\cite{Nussbaum2003Capabilities}.
From this perspective, implementing a desirable software property does not ensure that users can benefit from it.
For example, multilingual support may not enable access if the interface remains unusable or users lack the necessary technical knowledge, devices, or connectivity.

\section{Social purpose}
\label{sec:purpose}

Almost any software could benefit an organization with a social purpose: an NGO may use a spreadsheet, an accounting package, or a communication platform to organize its work.
Potential usefulness alone therefore provides little basis for distinguishing software with a social purpose.

Organizations pursuing social missions often need adaptations to common software because their structures, resources, and practices differ from those assumed by standard products.
A contact-management application, for instance, may need to represent beneficiaries, volunteers, partner organizations, and their relationships, with workflows and access rules suited to that setting.
Where beneficiaries or volunteers risk reprisals, protecting their identities may require pseudonyms, minimal collection of identifying information, and restricted access to sensitive records.
Other adaptations may accommodate intermittent volunteer participation or limited budgets.
These needs should be established with the organization and its communities.

These organizations also need software that directly supports their mission.
Examples include coordinating food distribution, matching volunteers with requests for assistance, managing access to shelters, or helping migrants navigate local services and administrative procedures.

This is what we mean by software with a \emph{social purpose}: software created or adapted to help organizations and communities pursuing social missions do their work better.
The connection to the mission should be explicit, identifying whose work or experience the software is intended to improve, which activities it supports, and what barriers it seeks to reduce.
NGOs are an important example, but the same reasoning applies to public services, cooperatives, informal community groups, and other organizations pursuing social goals.

A clearly stated purpose guides development and evaluation.
Evaluation could examine reductions in administrative effort, improvements in coordination, or completion of service-access tasks, alongside any new burdens or unintended harms.
A new project can therefore have a social purpose before its impact is established, provided that its intended contribution is concrete and its claims remain proportionate to the available evidence.

\section{A model-based development framework for \socialsource{}}
\label{sec:besser}

We propose integrating the three social dimensions into model-based development alongside functional requirements.
Just as models can specify an application's data, behavior, and interfaces and drive its generation, they could describe its community, expected societal qualities, and social purpose.
These specifications would connect social commitments to implementation decisions and support their review as the application evolves.

This approach could be implemented by extending existing modeling and low-code platforms such as BESSER~\cite{Alfonso2024BESSER}.\footnote{\url{https://besser-pearl.org/}}
BESSER provides an open-source foundation of modeling facilities and extensible generators on which additional social specifications could be built.
Graphical editors, forms, and natural-language interaction could allow participants with different technical backgrounds to contribute to and inspect these models before code is generated.

\subsection{Modeling social provenance}

The community specification would describe the profiles of contributors and intended users, their roles and responsibilities, and the governance structure through which they participate in decisions.
It should cover requirements work, translation, testing, and other non-coding contributions, as well as governance bodies, funding sources, and rules for accepting contributions and setting priorities.
Building on the Software Diversity Card concepts, these models could generate documentation such as a diversity card or a \texttt{governance.md} file.
This would keep project documentation consistent with the community model and make contributions, responsibilities, and decisions traceable.
Inclusive governance would still depend on the people involved.

Figure~\ref{fig:besser-user-profiles} illustrates a starting point for modeling the people around an application.
The editor captures personal information, language competence, and accessibility needs, with a conversational assistant helping construct an editable graphical model.
The example represents an older French-speaking user with vision-related reading difficulties.
The related work on personalized conversational agents shows how user profiles can inform the generation of adapted interactions~\cite{Conrardy2026Personalization}.
For \socialsource{}, this approach would need extensions for contributors, community representatives, roles, and governance; the figure illustrates only user profiling.

\subsection{Modeling societal qualities and trade-offs}

A complementary specification would capture expected societal qualities and their trade-offs.
It would connect the values discussed in Section~\ref{sec:qualities} to target communities, contextual requirements, priorities, minimum thresholds, and acceptable compromises.
For the migrant-services application, stakeholders could require keyboard access to essential tasks and protection of identifying information, while negotiating language coverage, operating costs, and support for limited connectivity.
A proposed domain-specific language (DSL) could distinguish mandatory constraints from negotiable preferences and record the rationale and participants behind each decision.

Beyond generating documentation, societal requirements would influence the generated application.
Deterministic transformations and templates could encode selected practices, such as keyboard-operable components, explicit labels, externalized text and language selection, data-minimization patterns, and appropriate access controls.
Resource requirements could guide implementation and deployment, while portability and maintainability requirements could guide the choice of standards and code structures.
Improving a generator could then propagate better practices across regenerated applications.
Supporting societal qualities \emph{by construction} thus means systematically implementing specified practices within a defined scope.

Generation alone does not establish compliance: repeatability does not imply correctness, and custom code, AI-generated additions, content, dependencies, and later changes can affect the result.
Accessibility and localization checks, privacy and security reviews, resource measurements, and testing with intended users would remain necessary.
Links between stakeholders, requirements, models, generated code, and deployed versions could connect this evidence to earlier decisions.

\subsection{Connecting social purpose to evaluation}

A social-purpose specification would identify the intended beneficiaries, the activities the application supports, the barriers it seeks to reduce, and the expected benefits.
Linking these aims to functional requirements and evaluation evidence would help distinguish intended contributions from observed outcomes.
A generated \socialsource{} Card could bring together this account, the community description, societal requirements, evidence, and limitations, with beneficiary feedback informing revisions.
Such documentation would support assessment; establishing social benefit would still require evaluation in use.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

The idea that software can contribute to society and the public good is well established.
Ferrario et al.~\cite{Ferrario2014SocialGood} explicitly advocate software engineering for positive social change and propose Speedplay, a framework combining agile development, action research, and participatory design.
Their work connects social purpose with development practices that account for the communities involved.
Abebe et al.~\cite{Abebe2020SocialChange} examine the roles computing can play in social change while emphasizing the limitations of technical interventions.
This perspective qualifies the opportunity offered by AI-assisted development: increasing our capacity to produce software does not, by itself, resolve social problems.
Humanitarian free and open-source software (HFOSS) is also an established precedent for open software intended to benefit people.
Ellis et al.~\cite{Ellis2015HFOSS} study students' participation in HFOSS projects, providing evidence about educational experiences rather than measured benefits for recipient communities.

Other work addresses the specification and modeling of particular societal qualities.
Moreira et al.~\cite{Moreira2023Sustainability} propose a catalog of social and technical sustainability requirements, represented through feature models and iStar, including concerns such as fairness.
Their catalog provides a precedent for translating broad concerns into reusable requirements and a potential input to the requirements DSLs envisaged for \socialsource{}.
Roy et al.~\cite{Roy2026Taxonomy} organize sustainability requirements across environmental, social, technical, and economic dimensions, discussing metrics and relationships between categories.
These relationships could inform the identification of compatible requirements and negotiation of trade-offs.
Our LangBiTe approach~\cite{Morales2024LangBiTe} offers a more focused example: a DSL for specifying ethical requirements and generating tests for fairness and bias in large language models, with traceability between requirements and their assessment.

For social provenance, the Software Diversity Card~\cite{R2} provides a direct foundation for modeling and reporting contributor diversity, an aspect less developed in the other approaches discussed here.
Related work on user modeling addresses a complementary concern: representing the people expected to use an application.
Our systematic literature review~\cite{Conrardy2025UserModeling} identifies fragmented approaches to user modeling in model-driven engineering, with limited coverage of user characteristics and tool support.
Such models can inform the profiling of community members, but describing users alone does not establish who participates in development, whose perspectives are represented, or who has decision-making authority.

These contributions provide foundations for \socialsource{}, and some already integrate several of its concerns.
Whittle et al.~\cite{Whittle2021HumanValues} further argue that human values need to be integrated into the software engineering method itself, rather than considered only as properties of the final software.
They illustrate how participatory agile practices can incorporate values into requirements rationale and development decisions throughout the software lifecycle.
Our proposed contribution brings social provenance, societal qualities, and social purpose together on an open-source foundation and connects them across the development lifecycle.
The accompanying research agenda aims to make this framework actionable within modeling and low-code platforms by linking community and governance models, societal requirements, and evidence of benefit.

\socialsource{} also complements the Digital Public Goods Alliance~\cite{DPGA}, which promotes the discovery and adoption of digital public goods through its registry, advocacy, and guidance, and maintains a standard for their recognition~\cite{R4}.
Our focus on software engineering methods could support these efforts by translating social aims into explicit models, development decisions, and implementations.

\section{Conclusions and roadmap}
\label{sec:discussion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\socialsource{} connects social provenance, societal qualities, and social purpose on an open-source foundation.
The hypothetical migrant-services application illustrates how these dimensions reinforce one another and what evidence their assessment would require.
We propose integrating them into model-based development through community and governance models, societal requirements, social-purpose specifications, and generators that support implementation and documentation.
Realizing and evaluating this framework requires progress on the following research challenges.

\subsection{Defining metrics and a social score}

A central challenge is to define metrics for each social dimension and use them to produce a meaningful \socialsource{} score.
Provenance metrics could examine participation, recognition, and influence over decisions; societal-quality metrics could assess properties such as accessibility, privacy, and resource use; and social-purpose metrics could examine alignment with community needs, adoption, and observed benefits.
Research is needed to select and validate indicators, normalize measurements, and determine how priorities should influence their weighting.
An overall score should retain the underlying dimension profiles and report evidence completeness and uncertainty, so that strong performance in one area does not conceal a critical failure in another.
Qualification thresholds and comparisons must also account for differences in project scale, maturity, deployment context, and available resources.

\subsection{Extending DSLs and integrating them into BESSER}

The framework requires DSLs that express all three dimensions and connect them to the application's functional models.
Existing languages for user profiles, diversity, governance, and software requirements provide starting points, but need extensions and integration to cover participation, decision-making, societal qualities, social missions, and expected evidence.
For governance, Ait et al.~\cite{Ait2026Governance} provide a DSL and decision engine for defining and enforcing policies in human--agent software development, including roles, participation rules, and traceable decisions.
This offers a foundation for expressing how communities participate in and govern \socialsource{} projects.
New DSLs should fill the remaining gaps and support the constraints, preferences, and decision rationales outlined in Section~\ref{sec:besser}.
Integrating these languages into BESSER would allow their models to guide generation, produce documentation, and maintain links to implementation and deployment.
The languages should support interfaces usable by participants who are not modeling experts.

\subsection{Building a library of socially aware generators and architectures}

A reusable library of socially aware generators, templates, and reference architectures could transform social requirements into implementations with documented guarantees.
Examples include accessible interface components, multilingual application structures, privacy-preserving data flows, and architectures suitable for modest devices or limited connectivity.
The longer-term objective is to support certified implementations for explicitly defined requirements, with each reusable element specifying its assumptions, supported properties, and verification evidence.
This requires methods for checking both individual components and their composition, since a property established for one component may not hold for the complete application.
Certification should identify the relevant version, configuration, and operating conditions, and specify which changes require renewed assessment.
Broader claims about fairness or social benefit would still depend on contextual and empirical evaluation.

\subsection{Negotiating priorities and acceptable trade-offs}

Expressing requirements does not resolve disagreements about which qualities matter most or whose preferences should prevail.
Research must develop participatory methods for eliciting priorities, representing affected communities, resolving conflicts, and revisiting decisions as circumstances change.
For example, a community may prefer locally maintained translations over an external AI service for sensitive material, while accepting narrower language coverage.
The process should make the rationale, costs, and consequences of such choices visible, preserve minimum requirements, and give participants meaningful influence over the outcome.
The challenge is to connect this deliberation to the models and tools without treating a recorded decision as sufficient evidence of legitimate participation.

Traceability should link trade-offs and their rationales to requirements, implementation choices, and evaluation evidence so that projects can revisit compromises as new information emerges.
Research should establish how a machine-readable \socialsource{} Card can maintain these links across governance records, models, source code, runtime checks, and user feedback.
It should distinguish declared commitments, implemented features, verified properties, and observed outcomes while keeping maintenance effort proportionate and protecting sensitive information.

\subsection{Making development tools and processes accessible}

Accessibility must extend to the tools and processes used to create the software, as well as to the resulting application.
Following the Capability Approach~\cite{Nussbaum2003Capabilities}, an invitation to participate is insufficient unless team members have effective opportunities to express requirements, inspect and modify models, test alternatives, and influence decisions.
Modeling environments, testing approaches, documentation, and collaboration practices must therefore accommodate participants with different abilities, languages, resources, and levels of technical knowledge.
One research direction is to explore voice interaction, conversational assistants, and augmented reality as alternative ways to create, inspect, and validate models.
Such interfaces could help non-technical participants contribute domain knowledge without first mastering a modeling language, while allowing them to inspect, correct, and challenge the tools' interpretations.
Research should establish which interaction modes work for which participants and tasks, and whether accessible alternatives enable effective participation throughout development.

\subsection{Evaluating usefulness and social benefit in practice}

The framework, its metrics, and its supporting tools require evaluation with organizations and communities pursuing different social missions.
Case studies should examine whether the approach helps participants express their needs, improves development decisions, and leads to useful software at an acceptable cost.
Comparing automated assessments with expert and community judgments would help identify disagreement, missing evidence, and misleading indicators.
Longitudinal studies should investigate adoption, benefits, unintended harms, and who remains excluded, while distinguishing the contribution of software from organizational changes or other interventions.
The findings should test the sufficiency of the proposed dimensions and guide revisions with the people the framework aims to serve.
